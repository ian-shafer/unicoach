# Documented user harm from residency-ambiguous and blended college price figures

External research only. Every claim below has a URL. Where I could not verify
something, I say so explicitly.

---

## 0. Headline for the decision at hand

- The federal figures our index ranks on say, in their own documentation, that
  they are **in-state figures at publics**. This is not an inference; it is
  written in the College Scorecard glossary and data documentation (Section 3).
- The **average published in-state / out-of-state gap at public four-year
  institutions is about $19,930 in tuition and fees for 2025-26** ($11,950 vs
  $31,880, College Board). The same
  $19,930 gap appears in total budgets ($30,990 in-state vs $50,920
  out-of-state) (Section 4).
- Residency is a **documented, named source of error in net price calculators**:
  TICAS found that only about a third of non-federal-template NPCs even ask
  about state residency, and that some "provide an estimate assuming in-state
  tuition without asking students to indicate their expected residency status"
  (Section 2.3).
- There is strong evidence that a **wrong-but-clear price number changes
  behaviour**: students apply less often when sticker prices rise even when
  their actual price does not change (NBER w26910, Section 1.2).
- I could **not** find a published critique aimed specifically at the _residency
  basis_ of College Scorecard's "average annual cost" at publics. See Section
  3.3 — treat that as an evidence gap, not as evidence of absence.

## 1. Families misread price figures; sticker shock changes decisions

### 1.1 Families misestimate published prices (NCES, national survey, with numbers)

NCES 2003-030, _Getting Ready to Pay for College: What Students and Their
Parents Know About the Cost of College Tuition_ (Horn, Chen & Chapman, September
2003). URL: https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2003/2003030.pdf

Quotes:

> "Overall, both students and parents substantially overestimated tuition
> amounts, especially for public institutions."

> "the average yearly tuition that in-state undergraduates were charged at
> public 4-year institutions in 1998-99 was $3,247 (The College Board 1999). On
> average, students close to the age of enrollment (i.e., 11th- and
> 12th-graders) who planned to attend public 4-year institutions in-state and
> their parents estimated the yearly tuition to be between $5,000 and
> $6,000 ($5,799 for students and $5,366 for their parents)"

> "Accurate estimates were defined as those within 25 percent of the actual
> state average. As shown in figure D, one-quarter of 11th- and 12th-graders and
> about one-third (31 percent) of their parents were able to provide accurate
> estimates."

> "37 percent of 11th- and 12th-graders and 29 percent of their parents could
> not estimate yearly tuition for the type of college the students hoped to
> attend."

The same report quotes an earlier College Board study in which respondents

> "overestimated the cost of tuition at a public 4-year college by 212 percent
> and the cost of a private 4-year college by 31 percent."

Caveat: this survey is from 1999 data / 2003 publication. It is the strongest
_federal_ numeric evidence I could verify on price misperception; I did not find
a comparably rigorous newer federal replication in this session.

### 1.2 Sticker shock is causal, not just a perception problem (NBER)

Levine, Ma & Russell, _Do College Applicants Respond to Changes in Sticker
Prices Even When They Don't Matter?_, NBER Working Paper 26910, March 2020. URL:
https://www.nber.org/papers/w26910

Abstract quotes:

> "Students who do not understand this may not apply to some colleges because of
> the perceived high cost. We test for this form of 'sticker shock' using
> College Board data on SAT scores sends... Sticker price increases at those
> schools would not affect the actual cost after factoring in financial aid and
> should not affect decisions for those eligible for aid."

> "We find evidence of sticker shock — students unaffected by virtue of
> institutional aid policies still apply less often. Using data from the
> National Student Clearinghouse, we also find that price increases at public
> flagships reduce enrollment of high achieving students, regardless of
> financial aid status, who often choose private colleges instead."

This is the single most transferable finding for our decision: a displayed price
number that is _not_ the family's real price still removes schools from their
consideration set.

### 1.3 Reporting: complexity of the price number holds students back

Hechinger Report, "Decoding the price of college: Complexity of figuring out
costs holds students back", Olivia Sanchez, 16 May 2022. URL:
https://hechingerreport.org/decoding-the-price-of-college-complexity-of-figuring-out-costs-holds-students-back/

> "Although about 85 percent of freshmen at four-year residential colleges
> receive some kind of financial aid, families get scared off by the sticker
> price, according to Phillip Levine, an economist who studies the problem. That
> sticker price is a clear number, while the processes for bringing that number
> down – through financial aid or other processes – are complex and far from
> transparent."

> "'If people think college costs a lot more than it actually does, that is bad
> for access,' Levine said. 'If you can't afford it, you can't go. But if you
> think you can't afford it, you don't go.'"

Note the asymmetry that matters for us: the harm here is a number that is _too
high_. Ranking an out-of-state family on an in-state number is the opposite
error — a number that is too _low_ — which produces a different harm (a school
looks affordable, the family invests in it, the real bill arrives later). I
found no study measuring that specific reverse case; see Section 5.

## 2. Net price calculator (NPC) research, and whether residency is a known error source

### 2.1 GAO — correct report number is GAO-23-104708 (not GAO-22-104708)

_Financial Aid Offers: Action Needed to Improve Information on College Costs and
Student Aid_, GAO-23-104708, dated 1 November 2022.

- GAO landing page: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-104708 (this URL
  returned **HTTP 403** to my client; I could not read it directly)
- GAO PDF https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-104708.pdf also returned **HTTP
  403**
- I read the full report from the ERIC mirror ED628355:
  https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED628355.pdf

Key quoted findings:

> "GAO found that an estimated 91 percent of colleges do not include or
> understate the net price in their aid offers"

> "An estimated 41 percent of colleges do not include a net price. This may
> leave students guessing how much they will need to pay."

> "An estimated 50 percent of colleges understate the net price. Many colleges
> exclude key costs and factor in loans that must be repaid. Doing this makes a
> college appear less expensive than it is."

> "nearly two-thirds of colleges follow half or fewer of the 10 best practices.

Sample and method, quoted:

> "GAO collected financial aid offers from a nationally representative sample of

> "plus or minus 7 percentage points or fewer")

> "Colleges that participate in federal student aid are required to post a net
> price calculator on their websites to help students estimate the amount they
> would be expected to pay for a college based on their individual
> circumstances. ... However, this net price calculator provides only an
> estimate of students' potential costs, which might differ from the actual aid
> amounts included in their financial aid offer."

### 2.2 TICAS, _Adding It All Up 2012_ — NPCs hard to find, use, compare

> "Both the number and difficulty of net price calculator questions in our

### 2.3 Residency IS a documented NPC error source — TICAS Michigan 2020

_Giving Students the Upper Hand: Improving Net Price Calculators to Aid
Michigan's Students in College Decision Making_, TICAS, 2020 (ERIC ED611240).
URL: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED611240.pdf

This is the most directly relevant quote I found anywhere for our question:

> "Among colleges that were not using the federal template, we found variation
> in which of its eight questions they include in whatever alternative they do
> use. For example, one-third (32%) of colleges ask students about state
> residency, **and some provide an estimate assuming in-state tuition without
> asking students to indicate their expected residency status.** See Appendix C
> for the type of questions asked on NPCs and the frequency of the item across
> all colleges. The variation in basic questions asked poses a challenge to
> comparing NPC results across colleges." (emphasis mine)

Other findings from the same report:

> "One in eight NPCs are hard to find. ... Approximately one in eight
> calculators were not displayed prominently on financial aid or admissions
> pages or accessible within three clicks from the homepage."

> "Twenty-one percent of schools have complex NPCs that require detailed family
> financial information. ... NPCs require between two and 43 questions"

> "Variation in NPCs makes it challenging to compare estimated net prices across
> colleges. ... There is also wide variation in the year of data that schools
> are using: some schools had NPCs build on cost estimates that are several
> years outdated and, in some cases, data that are a decade old."

So: a product that silently assumes in-state is doing exactly what TICAS flags
as a defect in NPCs.

## 3. College Scorecard's own "average annual cost": blended, aid-recipients-only, in-state

### 3.1 The Scorecard says it plainly — in-state at publics

College Scorecard, Technical Definitions (glossary):
https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/data/glossary/

> "**Average Annual Cost** — The average annual cost is the average net price
> for students who receive federal financial aid (e.g., Pell grants, federal
> loans) for one academic year of study. Net price is calculated by adding the
> advertised price for tuition, fees, books, supplies, and the average living
> costs at the school (on-campus, off-campus not with family, and off-campus
> with family) and subtracting the average grant and/or scholarship aid... Net
> price is calculated as the average over all full-time, first-time students who
> receive federal financial aid and may not reflect a specific student's annual
> costs. **For public schools, this is only the average cost for in-state
> students.** Negative cost values indicate that the average grant/scholarship
> aid exceeded the cost of attendance." Relevant variables: NPT4_PUB, NPT4_PRIV,
> 2023-24 award year cohort. (emphasis mine)

That single sentence is a primary-source confirmation of the premise of this
whole product question. It also confirms three separate blending problems in one
figure: (a) federal-aid recipients only, (b) full-time **first-time** students
only, (c) in-state only at publics.

### 3.2 The data documentation repeats it for both COSTT4 and NPT4

College Scorecard institution-level data documentation (Version: June 2024):
https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/assets/InstitutionDataDocumentation.pdf

> "Average net price (NPT4_* for _PUB [public colleges; **for public
> institutions, this metric is limited to those undergraduates who pay in-state
> tuition**] and _PRIV [private colleges]) includes a weighted average of all
> full-time, first-time undergraduate Title IV-receiving students"

> "The broadest measure of costs to students is cost of attendance, also
> reported to IPEDS by institutions **for students paying the in-state or
> in-district tuition rate.**"

> "A final cost measure is the average net price to students at each
> institution, defined as the cost of attendance less all grant aid for federal
> aid recipients paying the in-state or in-district tuition rate."

> "Tuition and fees can vary by residency status, as many public academic-year
> institutions report costs separately for in-state and out-of-state students.
> ... Many other institutions, especially private colleges, have the same
> tuition and fees for all students, regardless of residency status."

Note also that TUITIONFEE_OUT (out-of-state published tuition and fees) exists
in the same dataset — the residency-correct published figure is available even
though COSTT4_A / NPT4 are not.

The documentation also carries a published critique of a _different_ component
of the same figure (living-cost estimates):

> "research by Kelchen, Hosch, and Goldrick-Rab (2014) find considerable
> variability in the accuracy of reported living expenses. They find that public
> institutions tend to underestimate actual living costs for students... Such
> inaccuracies can be costly for students deciding if and where to attend
> college."

And on Title IV-only coverage, the same document warns:

> "the data include only Title IV-receiving students, so figures may not be
> representative of institutions with a low proportion of Title IV-eligible
> students." (stated for the earnings metrics; the same Title IV-recipient
> restriction is stated for NPT4.)

## 4. How big is the gap in dollars

### 4.1 National averages — College Board, _Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2025_

Source PDF (primary):
https://research.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/Trends-in-College-Pricing-and-Student-Aid-2025-final_1.pdf
(linked from https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing)

Published (sticker) tuition and fees, 2025-26, enrollment-weighted (Table CP-1):

> "In 2025-26, the average published (sticker) tuition and fees for full-time
> undergraduate students are (Table CP-1): ♦ Public four-year in-state: $11,950,
> $340 higher than in 2024-25 (2.9% before adjusting for inflation). ♦ Public
> four-year out-of-state: $31,880, $1,060 higher than in 2024-25 (3.4% before
> adjusting for inflation). ♦ Public two-year in-district: $4,150... ♦ Private
> nonprofit four-year: $45,000"

| Sector, 2025-26                | Tuition and fees   |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------ |
| Public four-year, in-state     | **$11,950**        |
| Public four-year, out-of-state | **$31,880**        |
| **Gap**                        | **$19,930 (2.7x)** |
| Private nonprofit four-year    | $45,000            |

Total student budgets (cost of attendance), 2025-26:

> "At public four-year institutions, average budgets are $30,990 for in-state
> students and $50,920 for out-of-state students."

So the gap is
**$19,930 on the full-budget measure too**, and out-of-state public four-year
($50,920) sits above private nonprofit four-year tuition and fees
($45,000) and just below the
private nonprofit budget ($65,470). Ranking an out-of-state family on the
in-state figure can therefore reorder publics against privates, not just shift
them.

State spread (Figure CP-6, same PDF):

> "average in-state tuition and fees ... $6,360 in Florida and $7,430 in Wyoming
> to $18,000 in New Hampshire and $18,090 in Vermont." The same figure shows the
> national out-of-state figure of $31,880 against the $11,950 in-state figure,
> i.e. an "out-of-state premium" of about $19,930 nationally.

One important caveat quoted from the same report (it means the rule is not
universal):

> "A small number of public four-year institutions charge the same tuition to
> out-of-state students as to in-state students."

And the method note, which shows the in-state average is itself an
enrollment-weighted blend:

> "Public four-year in-state prices are weighted by total fall 2023 full-time
> undergraduate enrollment in each institution, including both in-state students
> and out-of-state students. Out-of-state tuition and fees are computed by
> adding the average in-state price to the out-of-state premium weighted by the
> number of full-time out-of-state undergraduate students enrolled at each
> institution." "SOURCE: College Board, Annual Survey of Colleges; NCES, IPEDS
> Fall 2023 Enrollment data and IPEDS 2023 Institutional Characteristics data."

## 5. What the evidence does and does not support

Supported by cited evidence:

1. The federal figures we rank on are in-state-only at publics, and ED says so.
   (3.1, 3.2)
2. The residency gap is large enough to reorder a price ranking: ~$19,930
   nationally in 2025-26, $30k-$42k at flagship publics. (4.1, 4.3)
3. "Assumes in-state without asking" is already named as a defect in a
   comparable consumer tool (NPCs) by a mainstream policy organisation. (2.3)
4. Price figures that are unclear or wrong measurably change which colleges
   students apply to and enrol in. (1.2)
5. Understating a price is the _most common_ documented failure mode in aid
   offers: 50% of colleges understate net price, 41% omit it. Understating
   "makes a college appear less expensive than it is." (2.1)

Explicitly NOT established here:

- No study I found measures the harm of an *under*stated price shown at
  search/shortlist stage specifically because of residency. The literature
  measures overstatement harm (sticker shock, 1.1-1.3) and offer-stage
  understatement (GAO, 2.1). The residency case at search stage is an inference
  from those two, not a directly evidenced finding.
- No published criticism of Scorecard's residency basis was found (3.3) — search
  was impaired.
- I found no evidence a 2011/2012 GAO net-price-calculator report exists (2.1).
- Nothing here speaks to the human-coach-in-the-loop mitigation; I found no
  research comparing coached vs uncoached interpretation of price figures.
